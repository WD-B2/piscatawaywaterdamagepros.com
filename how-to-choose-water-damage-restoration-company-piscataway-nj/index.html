How to Choose a Water Damage Restoration Company in Piscataway

You will probably choose in a hurry, with water on the floor. These questions make that quick choice a good one.

Choose in the first call, not after the damage

When water is spreading, you may call the first number you find. A better approach takes about two minutes on the phone. Ask who will arrive, what they will do first, how they document the work and how they handle scope and price. Good answers are specific. Vague answers are a signal to keep looking.

This guide gives you the questions, the documents to expect and the red flags, so you can decide quickly, even at night.

Ten questions to ask on the phone

  • Do you answer 24/7, and what does same-day response mean when crews are available?
  • Are you licensed and insured, and can you show proof on request?
  • Do you follow the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard for water damage restoration?
  • Will you give me a moisture log and photos, and share them with my insurer?
  • What will you do first, and what will you do before you remove anything?
  • How do you explain scope and price, and when do I get it?
  • Who does the drywall and flooring repair, and is that a separate phase?
  • What happens if you find mold?
  • Do you handle permits for rebuild work, or tell me which are needed?
  • Who will be on site, and how do I reach them?

Listen for plain answers. A company that follows the industry standard will talk about water categories, drying goals and readings. One that talks only about price or speed may be selling a product rather than a process.

Mitigation and rebuild are different jobs

PhaseWhat it includesWho may do it
MitigationStopping damage: extraction, removal of wet materials, drying, documentationA restoration company
RebuildDrywall, flooring, paint, trim, cabinetsThe same company or a separate contractor
Source repairFixing the pipe, roof or applianceA plumber, roofer or appliance technician

Knowing the phases helps you compare offers. A fast, low mitigation price that excludes drying equipment days is not comparable to one that includes readings until the structure is dry. Ask what is included and how the company decides when to stop. See structural drying and dehumidification for what proper drying involves.

Red flags

Pressure to sign immediately

A reasonable company explains the scope and lets you read it.

No written scope

Verbal promises cannot be checked later.

No readings

If nobody measures moisture, nobody knows when drying is done.

Demanding full payment up front

Large deposits before work are a warning sign.

Be careful with claims that sound too good. We do not promise arrival times and we do not tell you what your insurer will approve. Insurers decide coverage. A trustworthy company says that plainly.

What to expect on arrival

A crew should introduce themselves, walk the affected area with you, ask about the source and take readings before moving equipment. They should explain what is coming out and why, and tell you how long equipment will run. Expect a written scope and a moisture log as the job progresses.

For commercial buildings, add a question about working around business hours and about the protocol for inventory and records. The commercial water damage restoration page describes how we handle that.

After a big storm, everyone is busy

After Ida in September 2021, Middlesex County received a federal disaster declaration, and restoration crews across the region were stretched. In a wide-area event, response times grow, and some companies take more jobs than they can staff. Ask what the schedule really looks like, and call early rather than waiting for the water to recede.

Reading the scope and the estimate

A written scope should list the rooms, the work in each, the equipment and the way drying is verified. It should say what is removed, what is dried and what is left for rebuild. Look for equipment days, since drying is a function of time, and look for the phrase that says how the company decides when to stop.

A line item that says only 'water damage restoration' with a single number is hard to compare. Ask for a breakdown, even a simple one. Ask what happens if conditions change, such as finding wet insulation behind a wall, and how change orders are approved. Written change orders protect both sides.

Finally, ask who owns the moisture log and photos. You should receive copies. They are your record, and your insurer will want them.

Choosing questions

Ask what licenses apply to the work, and confirm insurance. Requirements differ for restoration, plumbing and construction trades.
