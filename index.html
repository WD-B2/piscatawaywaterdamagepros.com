Who we are

Water damage restoration in Piscataway, NJ, day or night

Water damage restoration is the work of removing water, drying the structure and repairing what the water harmed. Piscataway Water Damage Pros does that work from a base on Centennial Avenue, with a 24/7 line and same-day response when crews are available. Most calls start the same way: a pipe, a roof, a drain or a storm, and a floor that is wetter every hour.

Emergency water damage restoration is time-sensitive. Drywall starts to lose strength within hours, wood begins to swell, and mold can start in a day or two when materials stay wet. If you are reading this with water on the floor, stop the source, keep clear of wet outlets and call the 24/7 line.

The process

How a restoration call works

1

Tell the dispatcher where the water is and what you have shut off. They book the earliest crew available.

2

Extract and protect

Crews pump out standing water and protect contents and floors.

3

Dry to a measured target

Air movers and LGR dehumidifiers run while moisture meters and thermal imaging track progress.

4

Repair

Removed drywall, flooring and finishes are rebuilt once the structure is dry.

We follow the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard for water damage restoration and ANSI/IICRC S520 where mold is involved. Both are published industry standards, and they set the vocabulary for water categories, drying goals and documentation.

Local causes

Why water damage happens here and what it means for you

Piscataway sits on the south side of the Raritan Valley in Middlesex County, and water arrives from several directions. Rivers and brooks flood low ground, aging plumbing fails inside walls, and storm rain finds its way through roofs and foundations. Each cause leaves a different mark and calls for a different plan.

Ida on September 1 and 2, 2021 flooded River Road and parts of the Raritan River corridor, and Middlesex County received a federal disaster declaration. The practical lesson is to know your flood status, since standard homeowners policies generally exclude flood. The Raritan crested again near Birchview Gardens on January 10, 2024, and Ambrose Brook flooded in 2018.

Supply water comes mainly from New Jersey American Water, and sewer service runs through the Middlesex County Utilities Authority. A 48-inch main leaked on Possumtown Road on August 2, 2021 and triggered a boil-water advisory. New Jersey American Water began a $2 million upgrade on March 26, 2026, so pressure changes and flushing can stress older fittings. After any local work, check taps, hoses and exposed pipes for new drips.

Piscataway is a township of named communities, and each has its own housing and drainage. Possumtown, Randolphville, Fellowship Farm, Society Hill, New Market, North Stelton and Lake Nelson sit on different ground, and Raritan Landing lies closest to the river. Rutgers' Busch and Livingston campuses, Robert Wood Johnson Medical School and the corridors along Interstate 287 exits 6 to 9 and Route 18 on Hoes Lane drive a lot of rental and commercial space. Landlords and tenants near campus often need clear moisture records, so we log readings by room.

The East Jersey Old Town Village in Johnson Park, the 1741 Cornelius Low House at 1225 River Road and the Metlar-Bodine House remind us how old parts of the township are, and older buildings often hide older plumbing and plaster.

Working with your insurance

Many homeowners policies cover sudden water damage, such as a burst pipe, and exclude flood and slow seepage. In New Jersey, the Department of Banking and Insurance rules at N.J.A.C. 11:2-17.6 and 17.7 expect an insurer to acknowledge a claim and begin investigating within 10 working days and to complete it within 30 calendar days of proof of loss. A good photo set and a moisture log keep that process simple. Insurers decide coverage, so call yours early.

Flood needs its own policy. Check your declarations page for National Flood Insurance Program cover or a private flood policy, and look up your address on the current FEMA map, since Middlesex County's Flood Insurance Study dates to July 6, 2010.
