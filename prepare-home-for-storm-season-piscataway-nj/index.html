Storm Season Home Prep for Piscataway Homeowners

A weekend of prep before the big storms can save a flooded basement. Here is what to check and what to write down.

Homeowner guidePiscataway, NJ

By the Piscataway Water Damage Pros team. Updated November 13, 2026.

What Ida taught us about preparing

Storm preparation is a short list that pays off the day the rain does not stop. Ida on September 1 and 2, 2021 flooded River Road and parts of the Raritan River corridor, and Middlesex County received a federal disaster declaration. People who had cleared gutters, tested sump pumps and photographed their homes recovered faster.

This guide is a prep list for Piscataway homeowners. It focuses on actions you can take in a weekend and on the records worth keeping before a storm, not after.

The outside checklist

ItemWhat to checkWhy
Gutters and downspoutsClear debris, extend discharge away from the foundationOverflow puts water against walls
GradingSoil should slope away from the houseWater follows slope
Window wellsRemove debris, check coversClogged wells flood basement windows
Roof and flashingLook for lifted shingles and gaps around chimneysSmall openings leak in wind-driven rain
Sump pump dischargeMake sure it flows away from the houseA pump that recirculates does little

Do the outside checks on a dry day with a ladder, or hire someone if you are not comfortable on a ladder. Falls cause more injuries than storms do. For roofs, a roofer's inspection is cheaper than interior repair.

The inside checklist

Test the sump pump

Pour a bucket of water into the pit and confirm it starts and discharges.

Find your shutoffs

Label the main water valve and the electrical panel.

Raise valuables

Move boxes and electronics off basement floors.

Check the battery backup

If your pump has one, test it, since storms often cut power.

If you have a finished basement, also think about what you would want to keep. Photos, documents and keepsakes belong on a high shelf or in a waterproof container. Furniture legs can go on blocks. These small steps limit what gets wet.

Records to make before the storm

Take dated photos or a video of each room, and of the exterior, before the season begins. Add photos of expensive items and of the roof from the ground. Store copies somewhere that does not depend on your phone. A before record makes a claim stronger and quicker.

Also call your insurance agent. Ask whether you have flood coverage, water backup coverage and a separate wind or storm deductible. Standard homeowners policies generally exclude flood, so ask about NFIP or a private flood policy, and ask early, since flood coverage usually has a waiting period.

During and after the storm

  • Stay out of floodwater, which can be contaminated and electrified.
  • Avoid driving on flooded roads.
  • Turn off power at the panel only if it is dry and you can reach it safely.
  • Photograph damage before you move anything.
  • Call your insurer, then a restoration crew.

When storm water does get in, the response is the same as for any wet structure: extract, dry and document. Our storm water damage cleanup page explains what we do, and flood damage cleanup covers river and brook flooding.

Access matters in a storm

During Ida, Washington Avenue in Milltown was closed by flooding, and River Road in Piscataway flooded. When roads close, crews take longer. The best prep for a storm response is to call early and to know a second route to your street.

Power outages and sump pumps

Storms often cut power, and a sump pump with no power is a bucket with a hole in it. A battery backup pump can run for hours, and some homeowners add a water-powered backup where the municipal water pressure allows. A plumber can advise on what fits your pit and your discharge line.

If you use a generator, run it outdoors and well away from windows and doors, since exhaust carries carbon monoxide. Never connect it to your panel without a proper transfer switch installed by an electrician. These are safety basics that apply every storm season.

Finally, keep a small kit near the basement door: flashlight, work gloves, boots, a few contractor bags and a phone charger. When water is rising, finding those items in the dark costs minutes you may not have.

What to do with the car, the yard and the shed

Storm prep is not only about the house. Move vehicles to higher ground if your street floods, and do not drive through standing water, since depth is hard to judge and engines are easily damaged. Bring in or tie down loose yard items, and store chemicals, fuel and fertilizer off the floor of a garage or shed so floodwater does not carry them around.

If a neighbor is elderly or lives alone, a quick check before the storm is a kind step. Share your phone number and ask for theirs. In a widespread storm, neighbors are often the first and fastest help.
